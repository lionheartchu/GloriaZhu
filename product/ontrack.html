OnTrack

A teen-first budgeting app that helps users make everyday save-or-spend decisions with lightweight AI guidance.

Bridging the gap between tracking and action, OnTrack redefines financial literacy for Gen Z by turning "I should save more" into concrete, daily choices.

View Prototype

01. The Problem

The Invisible Drain on Teen Wallets

Teens today handle real money earlier than ever—through bank accounts, payment apps, and digital wallets. Yet, over 70% of teens don't feel confident in their personal finance knowledge.

The core issue isn't massive debt; it's the invisible drain. Teens juggle many small, frequent purchases (snacks, gaming, rides) across multiple platforms. This makes their money feel invisible, turning budgeting into a stressful afterthought rather than an everyday habit.

The Opportunity

How might we help teens form early budgeting habits by turning abstract long-term saving into concrete, context-aware daily choices?

02. Research Insights

I conducted interviews with Gen Z participants (16-22) and audited existing finance tools to understand where the friction lies in everyday money management.

Insight 01

Invisible Spending

Small purchases are invisible until they add up. Teens rely on "mental math," leading to shock at the end of the week when balances drop unexpectedly.

Insight 02

Short-term > Long-term

Teens care more about immediate, short-term trade-offs than abstract "long-term wealth." Regret stems from "I shouldn't have bought that coffee," not "I didn't invest."

Insight 03

Friction Kills Habits

Existing finance tools feel too manual or adult-oriented. Teens quickly abandon apps that require strict, continuous categorization of every transaction.

03. Product Direction

What it is

  • Goal-first budgeting
  • Lightweight spend tracking
  • AI-assisted everyday trade-offs

What it is not

  • A traditional banking app
  • Dense investment education
  • A parent surveillance tool

Core Principles

  • Low effort, high awareness
  • Advice, not judgment
  • Concrete goals over abstract budgets

04. Bridging Research & Design

Research Insight

Teens stop using tools that require too much manual input.

Design Response

Hybrid spend tracking

Implement automatic tracking with lightweight manual corrections instead of heavy, mandatory categorization flows.

Research Insight

Saving is easier when tied to something concrete.

Design Response

Goal-first architecture

Make concrete goals (e.g., "New Keyboard", "Concert Tickets") the core organizing structure of the product, not generic spreadsheets.

Research Insight

Advice must feel optional and non-judgmental.

Design Response

Supportive AI framing

Frame the AI as "Ask OnTrack"—a supportive helper offering contextual alternatives, not a strict coach or parental figure.

05. Core Product Flows

Flow 01

Set up & Connect Payment

UX Goal: Build trust quickly while eliminating manual entry friction.

The onboarding process clearly explains read-only trust before asking users to link their payment methods. This ensures everyday spending is tracked automatically, which is crucial for retaining teen users who hate manual logging.

Flow 02

Monitor Weekly Budget & Goals

UX Goal: Shift focus from numbers to actionable progress.

Instead of a scary monthly pie chart, the Home tab highlights a "Safe to Spend" allowance based on the current week. The Goals tab visualizes saving progress in discrete, motivating stages (e.g., "Speedrun mode" vs. "Chill mode").

Flow 03

Ask OnTrack for Help

UX Goal: Provide on-the-spot decision support without lecturing.

When faced with a purchase decision, users can use the Ask entry point. The AI reads their current budget and goals to offer 2-3 concrete trade-off scenarios (e.g., "If you buy this, you'll reach your keyboard goal a week later").

06. Designing "Ask OnTrack"

Contextual Help, Not a Math Lecture

Why it exists: Teens don't just need static reports of what they already spent. They need help thinking through real, in-the-moment spending decisions.

What changed: The design shifts away from abstract finance advice ("save 20% of your income") toward contextual, goal-aware alternatives ("Get regular coffee instead of a latte today, and you stay on track for your concert ticket").

Tone matters: User testing showed that AI needed to feel like a supportive peer helper. It is explicitly designed *not* to be a coach, and *not* to be a parent. It presents options neutrally and lets the user make the final call.

07. Defining the MVP

To ensure the concept remained a feasible, focused product, I defined a strict MVP scope centered on the smallest usable loop for everyday budgeting.

Included

  • Onboarding & Payment Connection
  • Weekly Budget Dashboard
  • Goal Setting
  • Ask Feature (AI helper)
  • Weekly Recap & Activity
  • Basic Profile

Not Included

  • Investment Portfolio Tracking
  • Parent/Family Dashboards
  • Advanced Banking Transfers
  • Complex Analytics & Multi-month graphs

The "Why"

The MVP is strictly focused on building the habit of awareness. Adding investments or complex analytics would overwhelm the primary user, while parent dashboards would violate the principle of "teen autonomy" established in research.

09. Reflection & Next Steps

What Worked

  • Goal-first structure made the product significantly clearer and more motivating than a generic finance dashboard.
  • The AI helper shifted from a gimmick to a genuinely useful tool once framed as contextual decision support rather than a static coach.

What I'd Test Next

  • Whether teens prefer 100% auto-categorization or lightweight manual corrections.
  • What specific tone of AI advice yields the highest trust metrics over time.
  • Whether weekly recaps effectively drive long-term behavior change.

What I'd Improve

  • Simplify visual hierarchy further, especially on the Home dashboard.
  • Refine the balance between a soft "premium" UI and pure, lightweight usability.
  • Validate edge cases with a broader group of real teen users.
